Young and The Faithful

How many of us can remember a time someone talked down on what we’re capable of because of our youth? Maybe it was in school, coaches and teachers saying you’re too young to join that team. Maybe it was some of us that had older friends or siblings friends that would tell us, “You’re too young to hang out or understand us”. Maybe it was at work, I know for me when I was promoted it was a big deal. E.veryone doubted my ability to join leadership due to my age and lack of experience, both in the trade and in life. Or maybe for some of us, it’s been the Church that wanted to put you down for being younger.

This is the situation we see Timothy in and what Paul is encouraging him over today. We will see that while people may measure usefulness by age and experience, God calls young believers to become faithful examples worth following.

Main Point

God does not call young believers to wait until they are older; He calls them to become faithful examples through devotion, growth, and perseverance.

1. Faithful in Your Example (vv. 11–12)

11 Command and teach these things. 12 Let no one despise you for your youth, but set the believers an example in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity.

Paul begins by telling Timothy, “Command and teach these things.”

Timothy was not merely supposed to offer helpful suggestions or share his personal opinion. He was responsible for authoritatively teaching the truths Paul had given him.

“These things” refers to Paul’s previous instructions, especially the importance of rejecting false teaching and training oneself for godliness in verses 6–10.

However, Timothy’s authority did not come from his age, personality, experience, or position. His authority came from God’s Word. This is why Paul says:

“Let no one despise you for your youth.”

Memorize this one. Not just this part, but the whole thing. You’ll see why here in a minute but this verse relates to everyone in this room.

We have used this verse quite a few times to encourage ourselves when people in the church have treated us differently because of our age. Some of us are still treated as though our faith, service, or understanding of Scripture matters less simply because we are younger.

That is wrong, and Paul, more importantly God, does not approve of it.

(Physical age has nothing to do with spiritual age.)

But we must be careful not to stop reading halfway through the verse.

We love the words, “Let no one despise you for your youth,” but then Paul uses the word “but.”

We love and quote the first part, we gloss over the rest of it.

That “but” shows us that Timothy also had a responsibility.

Paul does not tell Timothy to demand respect like he deserved it. He does not give Timothy a clever argument to use against everyone who questions him like he’s always right. Instead, he tells Timothy to become an example.

It’s not a coincidence we see Paul give this to his disciple since he’s the man that also wrote 1 Corinthians 11:1

“Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ.”

Paul does not give Timothy a comeback. He gives him a command.

We have said many times that reputation matters—and it really does matter. Not everything people say about us will be true, but the way we live can either give credibility to their criticism or take credibility away from it.

Q: How could Timothy prevent people from looking down on him?

A: Timothy could not control what people thought about him, but he could refuse to give them a legitimate reason to dismiss his ministry. He was to demonstrate spiritual maturity by becoming an example to the believers.

The word translated “example” carries the idea of a pattern or model that others can follow. People should have been able to look at Timothy’s life and see what Christian maturity looked like.

Paul identifies five areas in which Timothy must be an example:

Speech — The words he speaks. This includes his conversations, jokes, disagreements, and how he speaks when he is angry. His words should reflect the truth he teaches.

James 1:19

19 Know this, my beloved brothers: let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger;

Ephesians 4:29

29 Let no corrupting talk come out of your mouths, but only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear.

Matthew 12:36

36 I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak,

Conduct — The way he lives. His behavior at church, at home, in public, and in private should be consistent with his profession of faith.

1 Peter 1:15-17

15 but as he who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct, 16 since it is written, “You shall be holy, for I am holy.” 17 And if you call on him as Father who judges impartially according to each one's deeds, conduct yourselves with fear throughout the time of your exile,

Love — The way he sacrificially treats others. Christian love is not limited to people who are easy to love. It is demonstrated through patience, service, forgiveness, sacrifice, and hard truth.

Romans 13:8

8 Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law.

Faith — His trust in God and faithfulness to God. Timothy needed to remain dependable and obedient, especially when ministry became difficult.

Hebrews 11:1

Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen.

Purity — His moral and sexual integrity. This includes his relationships, thoughts, motives, private behavior, and what he allowed into his mind.

Philippians 4:8

8 Finally, brothers, whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence, if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things.

1 Thessalonians 4:3-5

3 For this is the will of God, your sanctification:[] that you abstain from sexual immorality; 4 that each one of you know how to control his own body[] in holiness and honor, 5 not in the passion of lust like the Gentiles who do not know God;

Notice what Paul does not mention. He does not tell Timothy to impress people with his intelligence, personality, talent, platform, or even try to be right all the time, that would be pride.

Paul points to his character.

Before Timothy could call others to pursue godliness, they needed to see godliness being pursued in him. His life needed to agree with his teaching. He could not preach one thing and practice another.

This principle applies to every Christian, not only pastors.

Our age does not determine whether we can be spiritually mature. A young believer who faithfully follows Christ can become an example to someone much older. In the same way, growing older does not automatically make someone spiritually mature.

A birthday can make you older, but only godliness can make you mature.

Paul gives Titus a similar instruction in Titus 2:7,

“Show yourself in all respects to be a model of good works.”
Q: Does “Let no one despise your youth” mean Timothy should demand respect or go out of his way to manipulate respect from others?

A: No. Paul does not tell Timothy to defend himself or prove that he deserves respect. He tells Timothy to live in such a godly way that his character answers the criticism.

Some of you have been overlooked or treated differently because you are young. That can be discouraging, and sometimes it is completely unfair. But we cannot allow unfair treatment to make us bitter, arrogant, or careless.

We cannot say, “If they are not going to take me seriously, then why should I even try?” Nor should we say “if they are not going to take me seriously, I’m gonna make them”

Instead, we should live in such a way that our maturity becomes more noticeable than our youth.

We should not merely tell the church that young believers can make a difference. We should show them through the way we speak, the way we live, the way we love people, the way we remain faithful, and the way we pursue purity.

You cannot control whether someone immediately respects you. But you can control whether you are becoming someone worth following.

In any capacity, it takes leadership. What does that look like?

You do not overcome criticism merely by demanding that people respect you. You overcome it by becoming an example worth following.

2. Faithful with the Word and Your Gift (vv. 13–14)

13 Until I come, devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture, to exhortation, to teaching. 14 Do not neglect the gift you have, which was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you.

Paul begins verse 13 by saying, “Until I come.”

Paul intended to return, but until then, Timothy was responsible for faithfully leading the church. He could not place his ministry on hold while waiting for someone older or more experienced to arrive.

That connects directly with the previous verse. Timothy was young, but his youth did not remove his responsibility. Paul did not say, “Wait until I return, and I will handle everything.” He told Timothy what he needed to devote himself to while Paul was gone.

Faithfulness cannot be postponed until someone more experienced arrives. Waiting for someone better can become an excuse for neglecting what God has already placed in front of you.

Paul then gives Timothy three responsibilities:

The public reading of Scripture

Exhortation

Teaching

The word “devote” means that Timothy was to give these things his continual attention. They were not supposed to be occasional additions to the church’s gathering. Or when they felt like it. They were to remain central to his ministry.

The Public Reading of Scripture

The public reading of Scripture means exactly what it says: God’s Word was to be read aloud when the church gathered.

This was especially important because most people did not possess their own personal copy of Scripture. When the church gathered, they gathered to hear the Word of God. But public Scripture reading was not important only because copies were limited. It was important because the church needed to hear from God more than it needed to hear Timothy’s personal opinions.

Q: Why does Paul specifically command the public reading of Scripture?

A: Because God’s people needed to hear God’s Word together. Before Timothy explained or applied the text, the people first needed to hear what God had actually said.

Again, 2 Timothy 3:16-17

16 All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, 17 that the man of God[] may be complete, equipped for every good work.

Timothy may have been responsible for leading the church, but God’s Word remained the church’s final authority.

A church is not built upon the personality of its pastor, the preferences of its members, or the opinions of the culture. The church is built upon the truth of God’s Word.

That order still matters. We do not begin with what we feel, what culture says, or what we want the Bible to say.

We begin with the text.

Exhortation

The greek being paraklēsi meaning:

a calling near, summons, (esp. for help) [imploration], supplication, entreaty exhortation, admonition, encouragement consolation, comfort, solace; that which affords comfort or refreshment thus of the Messianic salvation (so the Rabbis call the Messiah the consoler, the comforter) persuasive discourse, stirring address instructive, admonitory, conciliatory, powerful hortatory discourse

Simple definition in context: calling someone to your side to encourage, comfort, or urge them forward

Exhortation means urging, encouraging, and calling people to respond to the truth. Making it applicable.

Timothy was not supposed to read Scripture and then send everyone home without explaining how it should affect their lives. He needed to call them to believe it, obey it, and continue living according to it.

Scripture does more than give us information. It confronts our sin, corrects our thinking, encourages us in suffering, and calls us to obedience.

Knowing what the Bible says is not the same as submitting to what the Bible says.

You can take notes, memorize verses, answer every question correctly, and still refuse to obey God. Exhortation takes the truth of Scripture and presses it against the life of the listener.

Teaching

Teaching means explaining the meaning of Scripture so that people can correctly understand it.

Teaching answers, “What does this mean, and why should we believe it?”

Application without proper teaching can become shallow or misleading. Teaching without application can fill someone’s mind without ever changing the way they live.

This balance in Hermeneutics and Homiletics.

Observation. Interpretation. Application

That is what Timothy was to devote himself to, and that is what a healthy church should remain devoted to today.

Paul then tells Timothy:

“Do not neglect the gift you have.”

The specific nature of Timothy’s gift is not directly identified in this verse. It was likely from my understanding and study to be connected to his calling and ability to lead, preach, and teach, but we should not claim more than the passage tells us.

What we do know is that God had given Timothy an ability and responsibility to serve the church—and Paul warned him not to neglect it.

To neglect something does not necessarily mean that you completely abandon it. Neglect usually happens gradually. A lot about that in this letter, slow unintentional (from being unintentional) drifting. We neglect something when we become distracted, fearful, lazy, discouraged, or inconsistent.

Timothy may have been tempted to shrink back because of his age, opposition, or the difficulty of leading the Ephesian church. Paul reminds him that the gift God gave him was meant to be used.

God’s gifts are not trophies to display or gloat. They are tools to use.

Paul says Timothy’s gift:

“was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you.”

This describes Timothy’s commissioning into ministry. Through prophecy, God’s calling upon Timothy had been revealed or confirmed. The elders then laid their hands on him as a public recognition of that calling and set him apart for ministry.

The elders were not the ultimate source of Timothy’s gift. The gift came from God. Their laying on of hands publicly recognized the work God had called Timothy to do.

This does not mean that every Christian will receive their own form of prophecy or have elders lay hands on them in the same manner Timothy experienced. These circumstances were unique to Timothy’s calling.

However, the principle still applies to every believer: God gives His people gifts and abilities to serve others, and He expects us to use what He has given us faithfully.

1 Peter 4:10 writes:

“As each has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God’s varied grace.”

Notice that Peter says to use your gift “to serve one another.”

Your gift is not primarily about gaining attention, building a name, or proving that you are important. God gives gifts for the good of His people and the glory of His name.

This is especially important for younger Christians. Some of us can become so focused on what we cannot do yet that we neglect what God has already given us to do now.

We think:

“I am too young.”
“I do not know enough.”
“Someone else could do it better.”
“I will serve when I become more confident.”

But God does not ask whether someone else could do it better. He asks whether you will be faithful with what He has entrusted to you.

You may still need training. You may still need correction. You may still have room to grow. Timothy certainly did—that is one reason Paul wrote this letter to him.

But needing to grow is not an excuse to neglect your responsibility.

Q: What does it look like to neglect a spiritual gift?

A: It can mean refusing opportunities to serve, failing to prepare, allowing fear to control us, constantly comparing ourselves with others, or using our abilities only for our own benefit/glory.

You do not need to wait until you feel completely ready before you begin serving God. If God has given you an opportunity, ability, or responsibility, develop it and use it faithfully.

Verse 13 emphasizes Timothy’s responsibility toward God’s Word. Verse 14 emphasizes his responsibility toward God’s gift.

He was to remain devoted to what God had spoken and faithful with what God had given him, as so do we.

3. Faithful in Growth and Perseverance (vv. 15–16)

15 Practice these things, immerse yourself in them,[] so that all may see your progress. 16 Keep a close watch on yourself and on the teaching. Persist in this, for by so doing you will save both yourself and your hearers.

Paul tells Timothy:

“Practice these things, immerse yourself in them.”

Timothy’s ministry was not supposed to be a side quest. It’s the main story. The phrase carries the idea of being absorbed in these responsibilities.

Why?

“So that all may see your progress.”

Again, being young receiving backlash for age or doubt from others in the faith. Reputation matters, someone's always watching.

Notice that Paul says progress, not perfection.

Timothy was a church leader, but he had not arrived. His growth needed to be visible. The congregation should have been able to see him becoming more faithful, mature, knowledgeable, loving, and godly. He’s setting the _______! (example)

This is encouraging because faithful ministry does not require pretending that we have no weaknesses. It requires continually growing through the grace of God.

Q: What two things must Timothy watch carefully?

A: His life and his doctrine.

“Keep a close watch on yourself” — Timothy must examine his character and conduct.
“And on the teaching” — He must guard the truthfulness of what he teaches.

Both matter.

Right doctrine with an ungodly life produces hypocrisy.

An outwardly moral life with false doctrine leads people away from the truth.

A faithful servant must guard both his character and his teaching.

Paul then says that by persevering, Timothy would “save both yourself and your hearers.”

Q: Does this mean Timothy could earn salvation for himself and others?

A: No. Scripture teaches that salvation is by God’s grace through faith in Christ, not through human effort (Ephesians 2:8–9). Paul means that Timothy’s faithful life and teaching would be God’s means of preserving him and bringing the gospel to those who heard him.

Timothy was not the source of salvation. Christ is the Savior. But Timothy had to faithfully proclaim the message through which people would hear and believe.

Romans 10:17

“Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ.”

This is why doctrine is not a small issue. What we believe and teach has eternal consequences. False teaching destroys, but the gospel points people to the only One who can save.

Do not spend your youth waiting to become useful to God. Spend it becoming the kind of Christian whose words can be trusted, whose life can be followed, whose progress can be seen, and whose faith refuses to quit. You may be young, but you are not too young to show others what Christ can do with a life fully surrendered to Him.

Application

Do not use your youth as an excuse for immaturity; become an example worth following.

Devote yourself to Scripture until God’s Word shapes both what you believe and how you live.

Use what God has given you now instead of waiting until you feel older, qualified, or confident.

Pursue visible progress, not pretend perfection, so others can see Christ changing you.

Guard both your character and your doctrine, because how you live and what you believe affect everyone who follows your example.

Reflective Questions

Does my conduct strengthen or weaken what I claim to believe?

In which of the five areas—speech, conduct, love, faith, or purity—do I most need to grow?

Am I using my spiritual gifts or neglecting them?

Is my spiritual progress visible?

Am I guarding both how I live and what I believe?
