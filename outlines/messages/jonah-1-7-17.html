Walk the Plank - Jonah 1:7-17

Introduction: You can think of a major power outage in a city. One person—like Jonah—accidentally causes a fault in the system, maybe a blown transformer. Suddenly, the whole city is in darkness. These people all walking together panic and begin to blame one another on who blew the lights. They question one man, but the man seems to not be bothered at all. Why? Just as in Jonah—Jonah's actions brought a storm to everyone, But when they question Jonah, he tells them who he belongs to. When they turn to the true source of light—God—they realize He's the only light they ever needed. In fact, once God was trusted, the city lights weren't even needed—they'd never needed them in the first place. God's light had been the guiding source all along.

Main point: Jonah realized his running had put others in danger, God used the storm to awaken compassion in Jonah, a call to live responsibly, and reveal a mercy big enough to rescue both the runaway prophet and the sailors.

Point 1: The Call we Ignore (7-8)

The practice of casting lots is mentioned seventy times in the Old Testament and seven times in the New Testament. In spite of the many references to casting lots in the Old Testament, nothing is known about the actual lots themselves. They could have been sticks of various lengths, flat stones like coins, or some kind of dice; but their exact nature is unknown. The closest modern practice to casting lots is likely flipping a coin.

It seems so mundane, but God uses these lots all throughout scripture. The practice of casting lots occurs most often in connection with the division of the land under Joshua (Joshua chapters 14-21), a procedure that God instructed the Israelites on several times in the book of Numbers. God allowed the Israelites to cast lots in order to determine His will for a given situation. Various offices and functions in the temple were also determined by lot. These are important events in history. Using what is seemingly like a coin flip is strange for these decisions. The thing that wasn't strange was they used it for God to use. Just as God does here. To get to Jonah and show him his disobedience and the effects of harsh discipline.

Look at Acts 1:15-26 for NT reference that might make casting lots make sense for new believers if they don't know a lot of OT references - Lilly

The panic and chaos was so loud. They were terrified, but still wanted all of this to be through. So after they casted lots, and it fell on Jonah, They were scared of him and in turn they asked him rapid fire questions.

Why? They believe the storm is supernatural - Sailors were experienced with storms, so when this one terrifies them (v.5), they know this isn't ordinary weather. After casting lots reveals Jonah as the cause (v.7), they assume a god is behind it. So they start investigating which god and what offense triggered it. All their questions are basically asking - "Which god do you belong to so we know who we're dealing with?"

Fear makes people search for truth. God is using the storm not just to Jonah, but to awaken pagan sailors to seek the truth. The questions aren't random, they are desperate theology.

Point 2: The Surrender we Need (9-12)

He says to them I am a Hebrew. That is him answering their questions. Then they said to him, "Tell us on whose account this evil has come upon us. What is your occupation? And where do you come from? What is your country? And of what people are you?"

Him saying he is Hebrew is telling them where he comes from, what country he resides, of what people he is from. Secondly, he is answering what his occupation is And I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land.

Charles Spurgon writes about this response saying, "That, I suppose, must be regarded as his occupation; and what a blessed occupation it is, — to be occupied with the fear of the Lord! So, you see that, though Jonah was not properly following his occupation while he was on board that ship, yet he did not hesitate to avow, "I am a Hebrew; and I fear the Lord, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land." The child of God, even when he gets where he ought not to be, if you test him and try him, will stand to his colors. He will confess that he is, after all, a servant of the living God."

Even if he was not being obedient, He still knows who he belongs to.

Verse 11, They knew they had to do something with Jonah to calm the storm. Since then they knew the reason for everything that has happened. We also receive the information that the storm is continuing to grow. They respect Jonah enough to ask what they should do. When I think of an ancient sailor I think of a rough crew that is aggressive and thievery. Prideful and selfish. I would have thought they would have handled it themselves after finding out what was happening, but this was not the case. Them asking Jonah was the confrontation of what happens next. The question was a reflection to Jonah and the running he was doing. Has someone ever asked you a question you have been forgetting to ask yourself? They ask, "What should we do with you?" God has shown them all this is the severe discipline to Jonah who has run. He then now realised what he would need to do.

Verse 12,

Jonah then decides that he has led all of these people through his storm and his time of running has come to an end. He tells them to make him walk the plank. Pick him up and throw him into the sea. Then and only then would the storm quiet down.

Why do you think Jonah wanted them to throw him in rather than throw himself?

It spares the sailors' conscience, In the ancient world, bloodguilt mattered deeply. By telling them to throw him in, Jonah is essentially saying: "I accept the blame. You won't be guilty. This is on me." He is taking responsibility publicly. Ironically, the pagan sailors become more morally sensitive than the prophet — they hesitate and try to row back instead (v.13). They even pray before throwing him in (v.14). So Jonah's command is also a legal and moral release for them.

It highlights God's sovereignty. If Jonah jumped in himself, it could look like suicide or self-determined fate. But when the sailors throw him in:

the storm stops instantly

they fear the Lord

they worship God

The focus shifts from Jonah's action to God's intervention. Jonah is finally starting toward surrender. This teaches us an important lesson. Coming to the end of ourselves, we can look forward to Jonah's prayer in chapter 2. Specifically in verse 2 and 3. "I called out to the Lord, out of my distress, and he answered me; out of the belly of Sheol I cried, and you heard my voice. 3 For you cast me into the deep, into the heart of the seas, and the flood surrounded me; all your breakers and your waves passed over me."

The only way for this to be possible was for God to intervene. Even after all of this, Jonah still does not show full obedience. He shows a heart prepared for surrender, but still refuses. The response should have been," do nothing, I must go and repent and everything will calm." Instead he decides he would rather float in an ocean rather than go and preach.

Point 3: The Compassion we Witness (13-16)

Just as we just spoke about (The bloodguilt) they did not want that burden. Sailors start to understand the one true God and his power, beginning to respect him. They row harder. They fight the storm. They try every human effort possible to save a disobedient prophet. That's compassion. These pagan sailors show more mercy toward Jonah than Jonah showed toward Nineveh.

Then their hearts soften and they come to the end of themselves. But when they finally see that their strength cannot override God's will, they surrender, pray to the Lord, and obey. They pray that God would have the same compassion and mercy that they had toward Jonah. Compassion or grace is one of God's most notable traits to most people. We as believers are called to be Christ-like. If I was to ask someone who knows you very well, would they say compassion is one of your traits?

They pray for God to not place Jonah's blood on their hands and that they would not perish. Now that they would start to turn toward the one true God, They are now calling on Yahweh. Note the Greek text from verse 6 to now verse 14.

They now pick him up and hurl him into the sea and the storm ceases. What power that would have been to witness. Their hearts were already turning. Well if they still somehow had any doubt I would think that would have also ceased just as the storm did. So much power, that they stood in exceeding awe of Yahweh. They have been transformed and decide to give an offering and make vows (a promise to God).
