Philemon Overview

We are diving into one of the shortest books in the entire Bible, Philemon.This is the 18th New Testament book found around Titus and Hebrews. This is one of the 4 prison epistles we hear from Paul the apostle, who is an incredible writer with the majority of the New Testament being written by him.

This is his shortest letter that he wrote while imprisoned in Rome bad in roughly 62 AD. Coming in at just under 350 words total, this book by word count is the 3rd shortest book in the bible, the shortest being 3rd John. This is one of those books that I personally didn’t know existed. We rarely hear about it in our church, or in street evangelism , or wherever we hear the word of God; Philemon is rarely mentioned.

Why do you think that is the case?

Possibly its personalness to who the letter is being written to. Possibly because of its directness/ less theological But does that make this letter any less significant? Absolutely not. 2 Timothy 3:16 says that “All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness,”

What’s special about this book is the writer Paul has written many letters to multiple church bodies that he visited during his missionary journeys, like the Ephesians, the Philippians, and the Colossians. And in each of these letters, he goes deep into building up the things that either the church as a whole was doing good in, the church as a whole needed to improve on, or just express how much he loved the church as whole.

All of his other letters had a collective group of people in mind. Yet this letter is not written to a church group, but instead is written directly to one individual; Philemon. Some of you are like me when first reading this. You might be just now learning that Philemon was not a church but actually a person.

So who is Philemon?

What significance does Philemon have to be included in the bible? What can we learn from such a short passage? Well Philemon was a wealthy Christian who lived in a nearby city of Colasse, which was roughly 100 miles away from Ephesus. Apphia and Archippus mentioned in verse 2 are believed to be Philemon’s wife and son.

And it is believed that his conversion to Christianity was through Paul; who was on one of his missionary journeys traveling through Colasse and Ephesus when Philiemon, who lived close between these 2 locations, must have heard Paul spreading the gospel and was converted through his message. After his conversion, he strived to contribute in some way, and his way was hosting Christian groups in his own home. Sound familiar? This is because during this time

there was no place for a church service to start, it was common for someone to host a group in their own home to meet and talk about God.

Philemon was also a slave owner, or an owner of bondservants, which was common during this time period. And one of his bondservants named Onesimus (O-nes-i-mis), wanted to be free, and fled away from Philemon, his master, to the Roman empire, possibly taking with him some of Philemon’s stolen goods or some of his property. And in his pursuit of trying to run away and hide away from his master, he of course runs into Paul the apostle, who is the writer of this letter and now a prisoner for spreading the gospel boldly in Rome. But despite this, Paul being Paul led Onesimus to find faith and trust in God, and helped convert him and become Chrsitian. Onesimus became a helper for Paul in as many ways as possible. And later down the line, as part of his new Christian lifestyle, he was to return and reconcile his relationship with his master.

Roman Culture

Now, in Roman culture, slaves were considered nothing or useless without a master or job. They were considered property of their masters. And many masters at the time would use their slaves as “pawns” in moving up in the hierarchy tree or for their own monetary value. The common punishment for a slave was death. And Paul knew that Onesimus’s wrongdoings dissolved his relationship with his master. So Paul needed some way to persuade, not demand, that Onesimus is useful to Philemon upon his return.

So Paul decides to write this letter basically pleading Onesimus’s case. Even though this letter is his shortest, his writing style still applies here.

Verses 1-7

He starts in verses 1-7 greeting him and expressing how thankful he is to hear about his love and faith for Christ and how it’s growing.

And he explains that through this love that Paul was “refreshed through you” (verse 7). Verses 8-22

He then in verses 8-22 makes his request to Philemon about Onesimus, asking him to forgive Onesimus and instead of returning as just a bondservant, he asks for him to return as a bondservant and more than a bondservant… a beloved brother in Christ.

We see in verse 11 he says, “(Formerly he was useless to you, but now he is indeed useful to you and to me.)”

And in verse 13 he says “I would have been glad to keep him with me, in order that he might serve me on your behalf during my imprisonment for the gospel,”.

Paul you see notices Onesimus not as a step stool to gain higher power or position, but as a companion and fellow brother who is helpful physically, and helpful spiritually for the advancement of the gospel.

This here can be stated as the start of Paul laying out the foundation to break the common Roman and Greek culture depiction of what a slaves and masters relationship looks like.

Paul also addresses the wrongdoings of Onesimus and his stealing by putting himself on the chopping block.

He says in verse 17 and 18 “So if you consider me your partner, receive him as you would receive me. If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account.”

That should sound familiar about someone important and their sacrifice.

And the last few verses are his final greetings and end of the letter.

Themes

As we dive deeper into this letter over the next few weeks we will notice a few distinct themes; forgiveness, reconciliation, and love. Which should be an eye opener for how in depth this scripture is that just off of these few verses we can obtain great application and knowledge of God’s word.

J Baxter

J Baxter puts it this way:

“As human beings you and I are God’s property; but as sinners we have robbed Him and are fugitives. Our guilt is great, and our penalty heavy. The Law condemns us. Conscience hunts us down. But if the Law condemns us, grace concedes us the right of appeal. As Onesimus found refuge with Paul, so we find refuge with Jesus, who, besides being the sinner’s Friend, is the co-equal Friend and partner of the One whose property we are.”
