Main Point:

A godly woman displays her faith through modest character, submission to God’s order, and faithful devotion to her God-given calling.

The Modesty of a Godly Woman (vv.9-10)

9 likewise also that women should adorn themselves in respectable apparel, with modesty and self-control, not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly attire, 10 but with what is proper for women who profess godliness—with good works.

The likewise we build off of the previous verse (V.8) where Paul desires “that in every place the men should pray, lifting holy hands without anger or quarreling; “

Like we discussed previously, Paul addressed the men at the time we’re caught up in heated debates over false teachings. In verse 8, Paul addresses men who were susceptible to anger and quarreling, commanding them instead to pray with holy hands. Then, leading us to verse 9, Paul turns to women and addresses temptations involving appearance, status, and outward display. In both cases, Paul corrects behavior inconsistent with godliness and orderly worship.

Commentary from , Ephesus was known for wealth, status, and the worship of Artemis. Some interpreters suggest that this background may help explain the emphasis on elaborate hairstyles, jewelry, and costly clothing. Whether Paul had the Artemis cult specifically in mind or the broader display of wealth within Roman society, his central concern is clear.

Yet, Paul tells them to instead to “adorn themselves in respectable apparel, with modesty and self-control” Adorn, greek being, kosmeō (kos-meh'-o) which means to put in order, arrange, make ready, prepare to ornament, adorn. Urging them to do so with modesty and self-control. Modesty has looked different from culture to culture throughout history because clothing styles change. What was considered ordinary dress in first-century Ephesus isn't what we wear today. But while fashions change, God's principle never does. The issue has never simply been fabric or fashion—it has always been the heart. Modesty asks, 'Am I drawing attention to myself, or am I pointing people to Christ?' It is an attitude of humility that refuses to seek admiration through outward appearance.

That means modesty isn't merely about avoiding revealing clothing. A person can be completely covered and still dress to impress, flaunt wealth, or seek attention. Likewise, someone can dress neatly and appropriately without making themselves the center of attention. Paul is addressing the motive behind the appearance just as much as the appearance itself.

Self-control here speaks of sound judgment and mastery over one's desires. Instead of asking, 'How can I get people to notice me?' the godly woman asks, 'How can I honor Christ? 10 but with what is proper for women who profess godliness—with good works.

You've heard the saying, "Reputation matters." What you say certainly matters, but how you live speaks even louder. Your profession introduces you, but your conduct proves you. Anyone can claim to follow Christ. Anyone can say they love God. But over time, your life will either give credibility to your words or undermine them.

Imagine telling everyone at work that you're the hardest worker on the team, yet you're constantly late, avoid responsibility, and leave others to pick up your slack. Before long, people stop believing what you say because your actions have built a different reputation.

Or imagine someone introducing themselves as an honest person, but they're constantly caught exaggerating or telling half-truths. Their reputation eventually overshadows their claims. This is exactly what Jesus taught in Matthew 5:16: "Let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven." The goal isn't to draw attention to ourselves but to live in such a way that our lives point people to Christ.

See this in 1 Peter 3:3-4 also, - 3 Do not let your adorning be external—the braiding of hair and the putting on of gold jewelry, or the clothing you wear— 4 but let your adorning be the hidden person of the heart with the imperishable beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, which in God's sight is very precious.

Paul is making that same point here. A woman who professes godliness should be known—not primarily for expensive clothing or outward beauty—but for a life filled with good works. Her reputation should naturally lead people to say, "She truly loves the Lord." Her actions become the evidence of the faith she professes. With faith comes obedience, which leads us to our next point…

The Submission of a Godly Woman (vv.11-12)

11 Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness. 12 I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet. This is one of the most controversial passages of Scripture in the church today. This passage raises two important questions: May women teach today? If so, in what capacity?

This passage is often approached primarily through emotion, rigid tradition, or changing cultural expectations. Our responsibility, however, is to understand it within its immediate context and within the teaching of Scripture as a whole.

Because Scripture has one divine Author, it does not ultimately contradict itself. Therefore, difficult passages should be interpreted in their immediate context and in harmony with the rest of Scripture. (Systematic Theology). 11 Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness.

Quietly, greek being, hēsychia (hay-soo-khee'-ah ) Strong’s meaning::

Stillness, quiet.

Precept Austin points out this does not mean “Speechless”, as that would derive from sigḗ, which means complete silence. It describes a peaceful, settled, and receptive posture under proper authority. Paul is commanding women to learn—something significant and honorable—while also specifying the order in which that learning takes place.

Again, remember the context. False teaching was troubling the Ephesian church, and some women appear to have been particularly affected by it (1 Timothy 5:13–15; 2 Timothy 3:6–7). 12 I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet.

Using scripture to interpret scripture for the sake of systematic theology, we get a lot of solid answers that can be broken down between what women can do in regards to teaching and what they can’t.

When women can teach:

Women may teach other women.

Titus 2:3–5 explicitly commands older women to teach and train younger women.

Women may teach children.

Timothy learned Scripture from his mother Eunice and grandmother Lois (2 Timothy 1:5; 3:14–15).

Women may help explain biblical truth privately.

Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos aside and “explained to him the way of God more accurately” (Acts 18:24–26). Significantly, this was personal instruction performed alongside her husband, not Priscilla assuming the governing office of the church.

Women may proclaim the gospel and testify about Christ.

Every believer shares in Christ’s Great Commission (Matthew 28:19–20; Acts 1:8). Women were also the first witnesses sent to announce Christ’s resurrection (Matthew 28:5–10; John 20:17–18).

Women may speak, pray, sing, encourage, and exercise spiritual gifts within biblical order.

Paul recognizes women praying and prophesying in 1 Corinthians 11:5. Colossians 3:16 commands the whole congregation to teach and admonish one another through the word of Christ.

When Woman may NOT teach:

Women may not hold the authoritative teaching office of elder or pastor. Elders are responsible for teaching sound doctrine, correcting error, and governing the church (1 Timothy 3:1–7; 5:17; Titus 1:5–9). Paul assigns this office to qualified men.

Women may not regularly preach or teach the authoritative exposition of Scripture to the gathered church when adult men are present. Paul joins “teach” with “exercise authority over a man” in the context of conduct within the assembled church (1 Timothy 2:11–12; 3:14–15). This is more specific than sharing a testimony, participating in discussion, or explaining truth privately.

Women may not exercise spiritual authority over the church’s adult men. This includes assuming the governing, doctrinal, and corrective authority belonging to elders (1 Timothy 2:12; Hebrews 13:17; 1 Peter 5:1–3).

Women may not function as pastors or elders without the title. Changing the title to “teacher,” “director,” or “ministry leader” does not resolve the issue if she is actually performing the elder’s authoritative teaching and governing responsibilities.

Women may not become the church’s final doctrinal authority. Elders are charged to protect sound doctrine, correct those who contradict it, and guard the congregation from false teaching (Acts 20:28–31; Titus 1:9). A woman may contribute biblical insight, but she should not occupy the office carrying final doctrinal responsibility over the church.

Women may not teach in a way that rejects or undermines the authority of qualified church leadership. No Christian should teach rebelliously, but this is especially relevant to Paul’s command that a woman learn “quietly with all submissiveness” (1 Timothy 2:11–12; compare Hebrews 13:17).

Additional insight from context,

Again, Women generally had fewer educational opportunities in the ancient world. However, Paul does not base his command upon educational inequality. As verses 13–14 will demonstrate later, he grounds it in something before all of this.

The order of the church should also remain consistent with God’s order for the family. Scripture assigns husbands a particular responsibility for loving, sacrificial leadership within marriage (Ephesians 5:22–33), while assigning the church’s shepherding and governing responsibility to qualified male elders (1 Timothy 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9). “Husband of one wife”. Although these are distinct spheres, they display a consistent pattern of male responsibility.

The Foundation of a Godly Woman (vv.13-15)

13 For Adam was formed first, then Eve; 14 and Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor. 15 Yet she will be saved through childbearing—if they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control.

“For” building off the previous point, in support of. Paul's doing then what we’re doing now, using scripture. More so, Paul's showing that this isn’t rooted in culture or his own bias, it started in creation. First came man, then woman. Paul does not end his instruction with verse 12. In verses 13–15, he explains why he has given this command and then reminds women that their God-given role is honorable and spiritually significant.

Some argue that Paul prohibited women from teaching because women in Ephesus were less educated or were being deceived by false teachers. Those things may provide relevant background, but they are not the reason Paul gives.

Paul begins verse 13 with the word “for,” connecting his command directly to the creation account:

“For Adam was formed first, then Eve.”

Paul reaches beyond the culture of Ephesus and returns to Genesis. Adam’s formation first does not mean that Adam was more valuable, more intelligent, or more loved by God. Both men and women were created equally in God’s image (Genesis 1:27), and both share equally in salvation through Christ (Galatians 3:28).

However, equality in value does not require sameness in responsibility. Adam was created first and was given God’s command before Eve was formed (Genesis 2:7, 15–18). Eve was then created as a helper fit for him (Genesis 2:18–23). Paul therefore sees the order of creation as establishing a pattern of male responsibility and leadership.

This distinction did not begin with the fall. It was part of God’s good creation before sin entered the world.

“and Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor.”

Paul then turns from the order of creation to the fall. The serpent approached Eve, deceived her, and persuaded her to eat from the forbidden tree. Adam then followed her into disobedience (Genesis 3:1–6).

Paul is not teaching that every woman is easily deceived or that women are less intelligent than men. Scripture places the ultimate responsibility for humanity’s fall upon Adam, not Eve (Romans 5:12–19; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22). Eve sinned through deception, while Adam appears to have sinned knowingly.

The point is that the fall involved a reversal of God’s created order. Eve acted independently of Adam’s leadership, Adam abandoned his responsibility, and both became transgressors. Paul uses this account to show why God’s order should not be overturned within the church.

This also means that the passage is not permission for men to dominate women. Adam’s failure demonstrates the seriousness of male responsibility. Biblical leadership is not a privilege for men to boast in; it is a responsibility for which they will answer to God.

“Yet she will be saved through childbearing—if they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control.”

Verse 15 is one of the most difficult verses in the passage. At first glance, it may sound as though a woman earns salvation by having children. That cannot be what Paul means.

Scripture consistently teaches that salvation is by grace alone through faith, not through childbirth or any other work (Ephesians 2:8–9; Titus 3:5). Not every woman marries or has children, and Paul elsewhere recognizes singleness as an honorable calling (1 Corinthians 7:7–8).

Therefore, “saved through childbearing” cannot mean that giving birth earns eternal life.

In this context, childbearing represents the distinct calling God ordinarily gives women within the home and family. Paul has prohibited women from assuming the authoritative teaching role assigned to men, but he does not present women as useless or spiritually inferior. Their God-given work is valuable, honorable, and essential.

The movement from “she” to “they” is also important. Paul moves from Eve to women generally:

“If they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control.”

Childbearing itself does not save them. Their continuing faith, love, holiness, and self-control demonstrates a genuine faith in Christ. Paul is not teaching salvation by works; he is describing the persevering life that accompanies salvation.

The word “saved” may also carry the idea of being delivered from the stigma associated with Eve’s transgression. Although Eve became a transgressor, womanhood is not defined only by Eve’s deception. God grants women an honorable calling through which faith, love, holiness, and self-control may be displayed.

This does not reduce every woman’s purpose to motherhood. Scripture honors unmarried women, childless women, widows, and women who serve Christ in many different ways. Rather, Paul refuses the idea that serving God requires women to abandon the distinctions God created between men and women.

Women do not need to occupy the pastoral office to be valuable to Christ or essential to His church.

Application:

Prepare yourself to carry the spiritual responsibilities God has entrusted to men.

Honor and encourage godly women.

If you desire marriage, seek a woman whose life displays godly character.

Do not repeat Adam’s failure through passive leadership and disobedience.
