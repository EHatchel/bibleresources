Conscience or Spirit? - 2 Timothy 1:1–5

Introduction

Imagine sitting in a prison cell knowing that your life is coming to an end.

You don’t know how many days you have left.

You have been abandoned by people you trusted. You have suffered for the Gospel. You have spent years preaching Christ.

And now, as Paul sits in prison, what does he think about? He thinks about Timothy. He remembers his tears. He longs to see him again. And then he remembers something else.

His faith. Not just any faith. A sincere faith.

A faith that had first been seen in his grandmother Lois, then in his mother Eunice, and now Paul says that he is convinced it lives in Timothy also.

What a picture of Christianity.

Paul wasn’t just concerned with what Timothy did. He was concerned with what was going on inside Timothy.

What kind of faith did he have? Was it real? Was it sincere? Was it genuine?

And that brings us to the question I want us to think about today:

Conscience or Spirit?

What is going on inside of us?

How do we know what is right and wrong?

How does our conscience work?

How does the Holy Spirit work?

And how do we make sure that what we are calling the Spirit is actually the Spirit of God and not simply our own feelings or our conscience?

Because Paul says in verse 3 that he serves God with a clear conscience, and then in verse 5 he talks about Timothy having a sincere faith.

So Paul is going to take us inside. He’s going to show us what genuine Christianity looks like—not just on the outside, but on the inside.

Point 1: Greet (v.1–2)

Paul, an apostle

I guess I need to put a definition because of the recent events. So let’s look into Acts 1:21–26.

So we see some qualifications here:

Walked with Jesus

Was there for His resurrection

Was an eyewitness of what Jesus had done

This is specifically the qualification given for the replacement of Judas in Acts 1.

Paul was an apostle in a unique way because he encountered the risen Christ and was personally commissioned by Him.

That is why I would say there are no modern apostles today. The apostles were uniquely appointed and foundational to the church.

Will of God

“Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God.”

The will of God = the promise of life in Christ. That is the ultimate will of the Father.

There are different ways Scripture speaks about the will of God:

God’s sovereign will — what God has determined and will accomplish.

God’s moral will — what God has commanded us to do and how He desires us to live.

God’s will for salvation — that people come to know Christ and have life in Him.

Paul’s apostleship was not something he chose for himself. It was according to the will of God.

From Paul, to Timothy

“From Paul, to Timothy” shows us the official history of this letter.

Paul continues with, “my beloved child.” Beloved means esteemed or dear. Son in the faith. Paul wasn’t his biological father, but there was a deep spiritual relationship between them.

Grace, mercy, peace

John MacArthur:

“If we want to truly motivate other believers, we must, like Paul, have genuine, loving, and unqualified concern for their full spiritual blessing. In addition, to their recognizing our authority under God, we want our brothers and sisters in Christ to know that they are loved by us without reservation.”

Just as said in the overview, many people deserted Paul. He finds great consolation and joy in Timothy. Their special bond, father and son, could only be separated by death.

Grace, peace, and mercy is the exact same greeting in 1 Timothy 1:2.

All of these words that coexist in Scripture lean on each other in perfect unity.

Grace is getting what we do not deserve.

Justice is getting what we do deserve.

Mercy is not getting what we do deserve.

Timothy must need all three as he continues Paul’s passed-on mission.

Paul wishes these for certain needs:

Grace to the guilty

Mercy to the helpless

Peace to the restless

In a majority of his epistles, Paul writes, “Grace and peace to you.”

When he writes to his pastoral ones, he adds mercy. He knows this is a great need.

Why?

James 3:1 — “Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers, for you know that we who teach will be judged with greater strictness.”

Mercy is especially needed for the teacher of God’s Word because there is greater responsibility and greater accountability.

Point 2: Think (V.3)

“I thank God whom I serve, as did my ancestors, with a clear conscience…”

Conscience

John Lennox has a conversation with Alex O’Connor where he discusses the difficulty of defining something that we all experience.

The basic idea is this: We may struggle to physically define the conscience or point to it as an object, but every person knows what it is like to have one.

The conscience is something within us that bears witness to our actions and gives us a sense of right and wrong.

Definition?

Inner man? The conscience is our inner moral awareness. But we need to make an important distinction:

Conscience or Spirit?

The conscience is not the Holy Spirit. The conscience is part of the human person. The Holy Spirit is God Himself dwelling in the believer.

The conscience can tell us that something is right or wrong, but the conscience is not an infallible guide.

It can be:

Clear

Weak

Defiled

Corrupted

Seared

We see this in 1 Timothy 4:2, where Paul speaks of people whose consciences are “seared.”

Consciousness is God’s thumbprint on man.

The conscience is evidence that God has given mankind a moral awareness.

Romans 1:20 says that God’s invisible attributes, eternal power, and divine nature are clearly perceived through what He has created, leaving mankind without excuse.

God has made Himself known through His creation. None is without excuse because of this verse.

Romans 2:14–15 is especially helpful when talking about the conscience.

Paul explains that even Gentiles who did not have the written Law could demonstrate that the work of the Law was written on their hearts, while their conscience bears witness. So there is a general revelation of God that all mankind receives.

So how do the Holy Spirit and conscience coincide?

They are different, but they can work together.

The Holy Spirit works through the Word of God to convict, teach, and lead the believer.

The conscience responds to what we understand to be right and wrong.

The Holy Spirit does not equal the conscience. The Holy Spirit can correct and shape our conscience according to God’s truth. That means our conscience should never be our final authority.

God’s Word is the authority. Our conscience should be trained and informed by God’s Word.

The conscience knows right from wrong from birth?

We are born with a conscience and a basic moral awareness.

Romans 2:14–15 shows that there is a moral awareness within mankind.

The Ten Commandments are written on the heart, but our conscience can be shaped by what we are taught, what we believe, and what we repeatedly choose to do.

At the moment of salvation, the conscience and the Holy Spirit are involved, but they do two very different things.

1. The conscience — recognizes the problem

Your conscience can tell you that something is wrong with your life.

You recognize:

“I am guilty.”

“I have sinned.”

“I am not right with God.”

“I need forgiveness.”

The conscience bears witness to your guilt, but the conscience cannot save you.

It can show you the problem, but it cannot provide the solution.

Think of it like a warning light on your dashboard. It tells you something is wrong, but it doesn’t fix the problem.

2. The Holy Spirit — reveals the solution and brings conviction

The Holy Spirit does something deeper.

Jesus says in John 16:8 that the Holy Spirit convicts the world concerning sin, righteousness, and judgment.

So the Holy Spirit takes the truth of God’s Word and brings conviction to the person.

The conscience says:

“I am guilty.”

The Holy Spirit says, through God’s truth:

“You are guilty of sin, and you need Christ.”

The conscience recognizes the guilt; the Holy Spirit points you to the truth about Christ.

3. At salvation, the Holy Spirit actually changes the person

This is where the distinction becomes really important.

The conscience doesn’t regenerate you.

The Holy Spirit regenerates you.

Titus 3:5 speaks of the “washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit.”

When a person genuinely comes to Christ, the Spirit gives new life.

So you could lay it out like this:

Conscience → recognizes guilt
 Word of God → reveals truth
 Holy Spirit → convicts and draws the sinner to Christ
 Faith in Christ → salvation
 Holy Spirit → gives new life / regeneration

And this connects really well to 2 Timothy 1:3

Paul says:

“I thank God whom I serve, as did my ancestors, with a clear conscience…”

Paul’s clear conscience wasn’t what saved him.

His conscience could testify concerning his life and service to God, but his salvation came through Jesus Christ.

And now, as a believer, the Holy Spirit continues working in him, shaping his conscience according to God’s truth.

“Your conscience can tell you that you are guilty, but your conscience cannot save you. The Holy Spirit takes the truth of God’s Word and convicts you of your sin, points you to Christ, and gives you new life. The conscience can expose the problem. Only Christ can solve the problem, and the Holy Spirit is the One who brings us to understand and embrace that truth.”

“Conscience or Spirit?” The title is a question, but the answer isn’t conscience or Spirit. It’s conscience and Spirit—but they are not the same thing, and they do not have the same role.

If you continue to ignore your conscience, you can sear it. As we see in 1 Timothy 4:2

'through the insincerity of liars whose consciences are seared, '

Have you ever gone against your conscience?

What did that look like?

Your conscience is not “your gut.” Your gut is a feeling.

Your conscience is your moral awareness concerning right and wrong. And even your conscience can be wrong if it has not been properly informed by God’s truth. The conscience is not the final judge.

God is.

Point 3: Remember (V.4–5)

Remember your tears

“Remembering your tears, I long to see you, that I may be filled with joy.”

There is no direct moment mentioned when Timothy cried. It is presented more broadly.

They had walked together for many years, so I could infer Timothy might have cried sometime between them.

Most scholars believe this would have been at one of their forceful departures. More than likely when Paul was taken from Timothy to be imprisoned.

“I long to see you”

Eagerness, soul longing, yearning, cravings.

A crave never leaves your mind, becoming fixated on it. Almost feeling empty until it is filled.

After Paul’s and Timothy’s separation, Paul yearns to see him again. What would become the final time.

Just as many left Paul, as previously mentioned, he only desired Timothy’s presence.

“That I may be filled with joy”

Joy can’t be crafted or created artificially by man. Joy can only come from the source. Paul can’t grab hold of it himself. He has to be filled.

Do we pray for people to be filled with joy?

The fruit of the Spirit comes from the Spirit.

I pray the Holy Spirit continues to produce these fruits in us daily, that we can continue to experience them always.

This shows us the importance of unity within the body and the fellowship of the saints.

I told you all the reasons I don’t like vacation. I don’t like it because it feels like a vacation away from my family. My home is where my brothers and sisters are. I don’t ever want to be away from the body. That is what makes traveling so difficult.

I feel isolated and alone. Right where the enemy wants me to be. I want to continue to remain strong. So I crave to be around my fellow believers.

Point 4: Sincere Faith (v.5)

When we look into verse 5, we see Paul say:

“I am reminded of your sincere faith…”

Why does Paul say he is reminded?

Paul is bringing Timothy’s faith back to his mind.

He is remembering something he already knows about Timothy.

He isn’t telling Timothy that he hopes his faith is sincere. He is saying that he has seen evidence of Timothy’s sincere faith.

The word Paul uses carries the idea of genuine, unhypocritical faith. It isn’t a performance. It isn’t an act. It isn’t Timothy playing a role. It is real.

What is considered a sincere faith?

Is it lacking in nothing?

Is it facing no doubt?

Is it being stable in all your ways?

Is it not living a double life?

Is it not living lukewarm?

Is it not serving two masters?

Sincere faith does not mean a faith that never struggles.

Sincere means genuine, real, without hypocrisy. A sincere faith is not pretending to be something that it isn’t. Timothy’s faith was genuine. It was the same kind of genuine faith that had been seen in his family.

Lois

“Which first dwelt in your grandmother Lois…”

Who was Lois?

Lois was Timothy’s grandmother. Paul identifies her as a woman who possessed sincere faith.

Eunice

“And your mother Eunice…”

Who was Eunice?

Eunice was Timothy’s mother. She also had sincere faith.

Acts 16:1 tells us that Eunice was a Jewish woman who was a believer, while Timothy’s father was a Greek.

2 Timothy 3:15 also tells us that Timothy had known the sacred writings from childhood. So we can see the importance of what Timothy was taught and shown from an early age.

“And now, I am sure, dwells in you as well.”

And now you, Timothy.

This is not a sharing of faith, where each member simply receives the exact same faith. Each person must have their own faith in Christ.

This is showing generational faithfulness. The faith was not inherited. It was instilled. Lois had sincere faith. Eunice had sincere faith. And now Timothy had sincere faith. They taught the truth and demonstrated the truth.

What Are We Passing Down?

Precept Austin gives an illustration under 2 Timothy 1:5 about heirlooms.

We give a lot of thought to what we pass on to our children.

Maybe it is a rifle that has been passed down through generations.

Maybe it is a piece of furniture.

Maybe it is a handmade quilt.

Maybe it is an old family Bible.

These things mean something because they have been passed down. But there are things more valuable than material possessions that we can pass down.

A good name. Honorable character. And most importantly, the example of faith in Jesus Christ. (preceptaustin.org) That is what we see with Lois, Eunice, and Timothy.

The importance of raising the next generation

This shows us the importance of raising the next generation in sincere faith.

We cannot give our children our salvation. We cannot give our children our faith. But we can teach them the truth.

We can demonstrate what sincere faith looks like. We can raise them in the Word. We can show them what it means to genuinely follow Christ.

The goal isn’t simply that our children know that we are Christians. The goal is that they come to genuinely know Christ. Grace does not run in the blood. But God’s grace can be seen through generations of faithful people.

Lois had sincere faith. Eunice had sincere faith. Timothy had sincere faith. The faith was not inherited. It was instilled. And now Timothy was responsible for living it out himself.

Main Point :

Genuine faith produces a clear conscience, lasting fellowship, and a life worth passing on.

Reflection questions:

If everything that is hidden inside of me—my conscience, my motives, my faith, and my relationship with Christ—were exposed today, would there be evidence of a sincere faith, or would I discover that I have only been living the appearance of one?

What am I passing down to the people watching my life—not just through what I say I believe, but through the way I actually live when no one is watching?
