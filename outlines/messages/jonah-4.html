A Perplexed Prophet and a Patient God - Jonah 4

There was a firefighter rushing into a burning building. The flames are spreading, smoke is everywhere, and people are trapped inside. He risks his life, fights through the smoke, and manages to rescue everyone. The fire is put out, and the entire building is saved.

You would expect the firefighter to be relieved, maybe even celebrated for what he did. But instead, imagine him walking outside, sitting on the curb, and becoming angry that the building didn't burn down.

That sounds strange, doesn't it?

Most of us would be confused. Why would someone be upset that lives were saved and destruction was avoided? But that is very similar to what we see when we come to chapter 4 of the Book of Jonah.

God had just done something incredible. The city of Nineveh heard Jonah's warning, turned from their evil ways, and God showed them mercy instead of destroying them. You would expect Jonah to be excited. His preaching worked. An entire city repented. But instead of celebrating, Jonah becomes angry.

Chapter 4 shows us something very real about the human heart. Sometimes we struggle when God shows mercy to people we think deserve judgment. In this chapter, God begins to teach Jonah, and us an important lesson about compassion, mercy, and the heart of God.

Point 1: The Anger that Reveals Jonah's Heart (v. 1-4)

Jonah is not upset at failure, he's upset at grace. God saves Nineveh, and Jonah burns with anger.

He quotes God's own character (Exodus 34:6) almost as a complaint.

His theology is right… but his heart is wrong.

Reasons Jonah May Have Wanted Nineveh Destroyed

Nineveh was Israel's enemy. Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian Empire, a violent nation that was an enemy of Israel. Its destruction would have been seen as a victory for Israel.

Jonah may have desired justice. The people of Nineveh were known for their wickedness and cruelty. Jonah likely believed they deserved God's judgment and wanted to see that punishment carried out.

Jonah may have been concerned about his credibility. Jonah had already prophesied that Nineveh would be destroyed. If God chose not to destroy the city, Jonah may have feared that people would think his prophecy was false.

This situation highlights the tension between human desire for judgment and God's willingness to show mercy when people repent.

When God's mercy toward others irritates us instead of inspiring us, it reveals that we love justice for others and mercy for ourselves.

God asks, "Do you do well to be angry?" not to shame Jonah, but to expose his heart

This is what God does all throughout scripture. Did you miss it?

God will ask questions to reveal our hearts.

Here is a few:

Gen. 3:9 "Where are you?" God invites Adam to confront his guilt and hiding.

Gen. 3:11 "Who told you that you were naked?" Exposes that Adam and Eve had disobeyed God.

Isaiah 6:8 "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Draws out Isaiah's willingness to serve.

1 Kings 19:9 "What are you doing here, Elijah?" Exposes Elijah's fear and discouragement.

John 20:15 "Whom do you seek?" Jesus speaking to Mary Magdalene at the tomb. Drawing out her grief and devotion.

John 21:15-17 "Do you love me?" Reveals Peter's heart and recommissions him.

We are constant, never stopping, forever working, not slowing down people. Stopping and reflecting on our lives is not something we do often, but it is something we should absolutely do. In most of these examples, these people were prideful. It took God to ask them a question to reveal their heart. Revealing that they are no longer relying on him.

This is something I preach about quite often. A sort of Gospel sharing technique. A lot of people you will come in contact with are people that have never been challenged. They sit around with people who don't want to make them angry or hurt their feelings so they never have to think too hard.

I know the God of the bible is my God. God uses questions all throughout the bible to get people to think and self-reflect. THE SAME GOD YOU HAVE IN YOU!

I'll ask these questions that are Holy Spirit inspired. To get them to think, be challenged, and self reflect. People have a lot of walls so more than likely not right then and there, but you would be surprised. I treat this just the same as planting seeds, I ask a question and I leave it open ended.

What's the purpose of your being? Not your goals in life, your purpose in being created. Do you hate God? Do you hate his church? Do you hate the people of his church? How often do you sit and think about how your life is going? Have you ever prayed before?

These may be considered uncomfortable questions, but this is a method of planting seeds for the gospel and is something I personally use. Sharing the same method God has for his believers to reflect on their heart.

Point 2: The Shelter that Reveals Our Priorities (v. 5-8)

Jonah leaves the city and builds a booth, hoping judgment might still fall. God appoints a plant for comfort.

Jonah rejoices over the plant.

God appoints a worm and a scorching wind.

Spurgon writes,"Those who are angry with God show the littleness of their minds. "Little things please little minds;" so a gourd made Jonah glad."

H.B. Charles Jr. shares a powerful message, "How Big is your God? If you have a big God, you only have little problems, If you have a little God, you only have big problems."

God provided a plant and it gave him comfort. This can teach us an important lesson: Temporary blessings can easily capture our hearts. He was well pleased with this plant and shade but in verse 1 he was well displeased when it came to the entirety of Nineveh.

Jonah is more passionate about his comfort than about 120,000 souls.

Sometimes God will disturb our comfort to expose our misplaced compassion. The plant becomes a mirror. What makes us rejoice? What makes us rage?

This connects powerfully to ministry, it's easy to care more about our inconvenience than people's salvation.

We get frustrated when, the AC breaks, plans change, our schedule gets interrupted, church becomes inconvenient, But do we get that stirred about people who are lost?

What inconveniences bother us more, than lost souls burden us?

What is your Ninivah? What is God calling you to? What is the purpose he is pulling you to in your life? Then once you have an answer for that, What comforts are you choosing over the will of God?

Or another question, are we rejoicing more over comfort, possessions, success, and convenience - Than we do over what God is doing in people's lives? If our greatest joy comes from temporary things, our priorities have drifted. Save that for a little while later this year lol.

God sometimes removes comfort to expose our hearts. God appointed the plant, but He also appointed the worm and the scorching wind. None of this was random.

We can easily apply this to our lives, Sometimes God allows discomfort to reveal:

our selfishness

our impatience

our lack of compassion

Discomfort often reveals what comfort was hiding.

When comfort disappears, what does our reaction reveal about our hearts?

Our emotional reactions reveal what we truly value

Jonah:

rejoiced greatly over the plant

wanted to die when it died

Application: What we celebrate and what devastates us reveals what has captured our hearts. You could say: "Your strongest emotions often point to your deepest priorities."

Point 3: The Question that Reveals God's Compassion (v. 9-11)

The book ends with a question. Jonah has pity on a plant he didn't create.

God has pity on a city He did create.

God contrasts:

Temporary plant vs. eternal souls

Personal comfort vs. divine compassion

Self-centered anger vs. sovereign mercy

Is it wrong to be frustrated with God? Yes, it is. Frustration is a product of our sinful human nature. Frustration with God could be evidence of a lack of trust in Him or a misunderstanding of who He is. If God is perfect—and the Bible says He is (Psalm 18:30)—then He has perfect intentions, perfect timing, perfect methods, and perfect results. As we focus our minds on the Lord and trust in Him, we will know peace, not frustration (Isaiah 26:3).

We continue to be frustrated, but we should read God's word. When we do, we realize that God is trustworthy and his promises are true. We can rely on him. Frustration could be a byproduct of our pride. When our pride disputes Gods purpose, we naturally become frustrated. Unless you want to end up like Saul and get flash banged and blind.

The final verse reminds us: 120,000 people who "do not know their right hand from their left", and many cattle.

God cares about people who don't even know they need Him. The book doesn't end with Jonah's repentance, it ends with God's heart.

There are 2 interpretations for this verse. Only one I believe makes true sense with the context of everything given.

The 120,000 refers to children, more than likely 5 and under, that literally doesn't know their right from left. This is a translation. With context and size of the city, this would have to be conjectured that the population would have to be 600,000. Which is not likely due to the magnitude of the city.

The 120,000 refers to the entirety of the population of Ninivah. Which they would be "not know their right from their left." This would mean in this interpretation they do not know right from wrong. Which makes the best vindication for this verse.

Gotquestions.org writes "Finally, it makes sense for the 120,000 to include all Ninevites because God showed compassion on the entire city, not just on its young children and animals.

Jonah 4:11 most likely refers to those who, spiritually speaking, cannot tell right from left. The verse shows that God takes pity on the spiritual blindness of the pagan. God's desire is to extend His salvation to all who repent and turn to Him."

Main Point

So the main point of the book is repentance and also Gods intervention, you see the story is named Jonah, but the book was always about God and his sovereignty.

Application

Are we angry because God is too harsh… or because He is too merciful? Do we share God's compassion… or Jonah's comfort?
